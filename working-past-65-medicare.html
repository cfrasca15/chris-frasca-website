Working Past 65: Do You Need to Sign Up for Medicare?

How the size of your employer, a Health Savings Account, COBRA, and your drug coverage affect when to sign up, in plain English and with every fact linked to its source.

The short version: If you or your spouse are still working and have health insurance through that job, you may be able to wait on Part B without a penalty. Whether you should depends mostly on the size of the employer, whether you have a Health Savings Account, and whether your drug coverage is as good as Medicare's. COBRA and retiree coverage don't count as coverage from a current job.

Start with the size of the employer

When you have both Medicare and a group health plan from a current job, one pays first and the other pays second. The size of the employer decides which.

The employer hasWho pays firstWhat it usually means
20 or more employeesThe group health plan pays first, Medicare pays secondYou may be able to wait on Part B while you or your spouse keep working.
Fewer than 20 employeesMedicare pays first, the group health plan pays secondYou will likely need Part A and Part B at 65.

Not sure which row you are in, or what it means for you? Call me. I'll help you work out whether to sign up now or wait, before a deadline passes.

Sources: Medicare.gov, Who pays first? and Working past 65

Part A: most people can take it at 65

Most people pay no premium for Part A (hospital insurance). If that is you, you can sign up when you turn 65 or any time later. The main reason to hold off is a Health Savings Account.

If you sign up later for Part A with no premium, your Part A coverage starts 6 months back from when you sign up or apply for Social Security benefits. It can't start earlier than the month you turned 65.

Sources: Medicare.gov, Working past 65 and When does Medicare coverage start?

If you have a Health Savings Account

Once you are enrolled in any part of Medicare, you can't contribute to an HSA for the months after your Medicare start date. But being eligible for Medicare is not the same as being enrolled. If you are still covered by an HSA-qualified health plan, and you hold off on both Medicare and Social Security benefits, you can keep contributing after 65.

What you need to do depends on when you sign up.

  • You sign up as you turn 65. Your Medicare starts the month you turn 65, or the month before if your birthday is on the 1st. There is no look-back to worry about. You can contribute for the months before Medicare starts, so your limit for that year is prorated.
  • You sign up after 65. This is where the 6-month rule comes in. Part A with no premium goes back up to 6 months from the day you sign up or apply for Social Security benefits, but never earlier than the month you turned 65. Stop your HSA contributions, and your employer's, 6 months before you apply. Otherwise the contributions made in those months can leave you with a tax penalty.
  • You already get Social Security before 65. You are enrolled in Medicare automatically when you turn 65, and your HSA contributions stop then.

Prorating the year Medicare starts

Take your yearly contribution limit, add the $1,000 catch-up amount if you are 55 or older, then multiply by the number of months before your Medicare start date and divide by 12.

Example: Bob turns 65 on October 7 and enrolls in Medicare, so his Medicare starts October 1. He was eligible to contribute for 9 months, January through September, so he can put in 9/12 of his yearly limit, including the catch-up amount. He has until the tax filing deadline the next April to make that contribution.

A few more HSA points

  • Your spouse's Medicare doesn't stop your contributions. Your eligibility is based on your own status as the HSA owner. If your spouse enrolls in Medicare and you don't, you can still contribute to your own HSA.
  • The money is still yours to use. After you enroll in Medicare, you can keep spending your HSA on qualified medical expenses without paying tax on it.
  • You can use it for most Medicare premiums. That includes premiums for Part B, Part D, and Medicare Advantage plans. Medigap premiums don't count as a qualified expense.

HSA rules are tax rules, and I'm not a tax advisor. I can help you with the Medicare timing, and a tax professional can confirm your contribution amounts.

Sources: Medicare.gov, Working past 65 and When does Medicare coverage start?; IRS, Publication 969, Health Savings Accounts

Part B: when you can wait, and your 8-month window

  • You may be able to wait. With health insurance from a job, you can wait until you or your spouse stop working to sign up for Part B, and you won't pay a late enrollment penalty.
  • Then you have 8 months. Once you stop working, or lose that insurance if that happens first, you have an 8-month Special Enrollment Period to sign up.
  • Don't wait the full 8 months on drug coverage. The 8 months are for Part B. Drug coverage has a shorter clock. If you go 63 days or more without Medicare drug coverage or other creditable drug coverage, you may pay a Part D late enrollment penalty. So line up your drug coverage within about two months of losing your employer plan, even though Part B gives you longer. More on that below.
  • Coverage generally starts the month after you sign up. If you sign up while you or your spouse are still working, you can ask to delay your Part B start date up to 3 months, which helps you line it up with the day your job coverage ends.
  • What Part B costs. The standard Part B premium is $209.40 each month in 2027, or higher depending on your income.
  • If you miss the window. In most cases you pay a late enrollment penalty for as long as you have Part B. It adds 10% to the premium for each full 12-month period you could have had Part B but didn't.

Sources: Medicare.gov, Working past 65, When does Medicare coverage start?, Medicare costs and Costs for Medicare drug coverage; CMS, Enrolling in Medicare Part A & Part B

COBRA and retiree coverage don't buy you time

  • COBRA doesn't extend your window. Your 8 months start when you stop working or lose insurance, even if you choose COBRA. COBRA isn't considered group health plan coverage.
  • COBRA will probably end once you sign up for Medicare. If you take COBRA after you already have Medicare, COBRA pays after Medicare.
  • Retiree coverage may expect you to have Medicare. Retiree coverage may not pay for your health services if you don't have both Part A and Part B. If you have retiree coverage, I'll help you find out how it works with Medicare before you decide.
  • COBRA or retiree coverage ending is not a second chance. It is one of the situations that don't qualify for a Special Enrollment Period.

Sources: Medicare.gov, Working past 65 and When does Medicare coverage start?

Drug coverage has its own clock

Medicare calls drug coverage "creditable" when it is similar in value to Medicare drug coverage (Part D). Your employer plan sends a notice each year that says whether its drug coverage is creditable. Keep it, and I can help you read it.

If you go 63 days or more without Medicare drug coverage or other creditable drug coverage after you are first eligible, you may pay a Part D late enrollment penalty. It is calculated by multiplying 1% of the national base beneficiary premium, $41.33 in 2027, by the number of full months you went without coverage.

Sources: Medicare.gov, Costs for Medicare drug coverage and Medicare costs

Covered by your spouse's job?

The same rules apply. If you or your spouse are still working, you may be able to wait to sign up. What matters is that the coverage comes from a current job, and how large that employer is.

If your coverage is not from a job at all, like a Covered California plan, different rules apply. See moving from Covered California to Medicare.

Source: Medicare.gov, Working past 65

Getting ready to retire: a checklist

  • If you have an HSA and you are signing up after 65, stop HSA contributions 6 months before you apply for Medicare or Social Security benefits.
  • Two to three months before you retire, pin down the exact day your job coverage ends.
  • Get form CMS-L564, Request for Employment Information, filled out. It is the proof of group health coverage Social Security needs when you sign up for Part B after 65. You complete one section and your employer completes the other, and it goes in with your Part B application, form CMS-40B. I'll walk you through both.
  • Line up drug coverage within 63 days of losing your employer drug coverage, so you don't face a Part D late enrollment penalty.
  • Sign up for Part B so it starts when your job coverage ends, with no gap.
  • Don't count on COBRA or retiree coverage to give you more time.
  • Decide how you want to get your Medicare coverage, such as Original Medicare with Medigap and a Part D drug plan, or a Medicare Advantage plan. See the differences.
  • If you want a Medigap policy, know that your one-time 6-month Medigap enrollment window starts the first month you are 65 or older and signed up for Part B.
  • Write down your doctors, hospital, medications, and pharmacy before you review plan options.

Sources: Medicare.gov, Enrollment forms and When can I buy a Medigap policy? For the basics of your first enrollment window, see my turning 65 guide.

Common questions

Do I have to sign up for Medicare at 65 if I am still working?

Not always. If you or your spouse are still working and have health insurance through that job, you may be able to wait on Part B without a late enrollment penalty. It depends mostly on the size of the employer, and I can help you work out which rule applies to you.

Why does the size of my employer matter?

It decides who pays first. With 20 or more employees, the group health plan pays first and Medicare pays second. With fewer than 20 employees, Medicare pays first and the group health plan pays second.

Can I keep contributing to my Health Savings Account?

Yes, until you are enrolled in Medicare. If you are still on an HSA-qualified health plan and you hold off on both Medicare and Social Security benefits, you can keep contributing after 65. The 6-month rule applies when you sign up after 65: Part A with no premium can go back up to 6 months, so stop contributing 6 months before you apply.

Does COBRA count as employer coverage?

No. COBRA isn't considered group health plan coverage and doesn't extend your time to sign up for Medicare. Your 8-month window starts when you stop working or lose the job coverage, even if you choose COBRA.

How long do I have to sign up after I retire?

You have an 8-month Special Enrollment Period for Part B that starts when you stop working or lose the job coverage, whichever happens first. Don't wait that long on drug coverage: going 63 days or more without creditable drug coverage can lead to a Part D late enrollment penalty.
