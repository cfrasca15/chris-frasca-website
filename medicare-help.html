Common questions

Medicare questions, answered simply

When can I enroll in Medicare?

Your Initial Enrollment Period starts 3 months before the month you turn 65 and ends 3 months after — a 7-month window. If you miss it, the Annual Enrollment Period (October 15 – December 7 each year) lets you make changes, and Medicare Advantage plans have their own Open Enrollment Period from January 1 – March 31.

What's the difference between Medicare Advantage and Medigap?

Medicare Advantage (Part C) bundles your hospital, medical, and usually drug coverage into one plan through a private insurer, often with extra benefits like dental or vision. Medigap (Medicare Supplement) instead works alongside Original Medicare to help cover the gaps — deductibles, copays, coinsurance — but is sold separately from drug coverage. Which fits better depends on your doctors, budget, and how you like to receive care.

Does Medicare cover prescription drugs?

Original Medicare (Parts A and B) doesn't cover most prescription drugs on its own. You'll need a standalone Part D plan, or a Medicare Advantage plan that includes drug coverage, to be protected against prescription costs and the Part D late enrollment penalty.

What does it cost to work with you?

Nothing. Licensed insurance agents are paid a commission by the insurance carrier, not by you — your premium is exactly the same whether you enroll through me, a different agent, or directly with Medicare. There's no fee for a consultation, plan comparison, or ongoing service.

I'm still working at 65 — do I need to sign up for Medicare?

It depends on the size of your employer and your current coverage. If your employer has 20 or more employees, you can often delay Part B without a penalty. If it has fewer than 20, you likely need to enroll in Medicare right away. This is exactly the kind of decision worth a quick, free conversation before you make a mistake that's hard to undo.

When is the Medicare Annual Enrollment Period (AEP)?

AEP runs October 15 through December 7 every year. This is when anyone with Medicare can switch Medicare Advantage plans, switch Part D drug plans, or move between Original Medicare and Medicare Advantage, with changes taking effect January 1.

What happens if my Medicare Advantage plan is discontinued in Orange County?

Insurance carriers sometimes discontinue a Medicare Advantage plan or shrink their service area for the next plan year. If that happens to your plan, Medicare requires the carrier to notify you in writing, and you automatically qualify for a Special Enrollment Period to pick a new plan — you won't lose coverage in the gap, but you do need to actively choose a replacement rather than waiting for one to be assigned.

My doctor's medical group is no longer in-network with my plan — what are my options?

This happens when a health plan and a hospital system or medical group can't agree on a new contract, and it can affect your coverage even outside the usual enrollment windows in some cases. Depending on your specific plan and situation, you may qualify for a Special Enrollment Period to switch to a plan that keeps your doctors in-network — worth a quick, free network check before you assume you have to change doctors.

Are PPO Medicare Advantage plans becoming harder to find in Orange County?

Some carriers have been reducing or discontinuing PPO options in certain counties in recent years, shifting more of their offerings toward HMO plans. PPOs generally offer more flexibility to see out-of-network providers, so if your PPO plan is affected, it's worth comparing what's still available rather than defaulting to an HMO without checking your specific doctors and needs.

Why did I get a letter saying my Medicare plan is ending or changing?

That's called an Annual Notice of Change (ANOC) or a non-renewal notice, and carriers are required to send it every fall if your plan's costs, benefits, network, or availability are changing for the next year — or if the plan won't be offered at all. It's worth reading closely and comparing it against other options before your enrollment deadline, since sticking with the status quo isn't always the best — or even available — choice.
